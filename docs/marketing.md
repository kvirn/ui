# Go-to-market: who buys KvirnUI, and how the landing page should speak to them

> **Internal.** Written 2026-10-08 for the `ux-designer` redesigning `/` (apps/docs). Other libraries are named here for analysis only and never in public copy. Public voice: kind, calm, concrete; we never disparage or name anyone. Not legal advice. Every claim is marked **[sourced]** (link in §9, `S#`) or **[assumption]** (a hypothesis to test with the first ten conversations). Wording limits come from `.claude/skills/regulations/SKILL.md`: say "designed and tested to meet WCAG 2.2 AA", never "compliant", "certified" or "fully accessible".

## 1. Positioning

**In one paragraph.** KvirnUI is the headless React library for teams who build public services in the Nordics and the EU and have to show, not just say, that those services work for everyone. It does the invisible parts (keyboard, focus, screen-reader announcements, contrast, six languages) and leaves the markup and the brand to you. What sets it apart is that the evidence sits beside every claim: a written contract for each component, a test for each key, axe checks in every theme, and an open status label that says "pending" where real assistive-technology testing has not happened yet. The code is published under the AGPL; organisations that cannot or will not publish their source license it commercially. **[sourced: repo, README, LICENSING.md]**

**Why this is a real gap.** Buyers are told by law to ask for accessibility in tenders (S1, S19), the obligation stays with the buyer when a supplier builds the service (S13), and measurement shows how far there is to go: a 2026 PTS measurement of Swedish sites found that fewer than one in five public sites had no automatically detectable failure (S5). Yet libraries rarely publish a per-component conformance report; one author who wrote one for a library noted almost no component vendor does (S40). **[sourced]**

**Tagline test.** Judge each on three questions: is it true today, does a decision maker understand it, does a developer feel respected?

Navigating the web without it is harder.") is warm and true, and it can stay as the emotional line further down; as a headline it does not yet say what we sell or to whom, and it could come from any library. **[assumption: to test in the §9 usability test of the design spec]**

**The law that creates demand.** WAD plus national law (SE DOS-lagen 2018:1937, FI 306/2019, NO forskrift) for the public sector; the EAA from 28 June 2025 for private services (SE LPTT 2023:254, supervised by PTS, which began monitoring in Oct 2025 with a small initial scope of large companies; FI via STM). Supervision moves: Digg is still the DOS supervisor in June 2026, and the government has told PTS to prepare taking over Digg's tasks by **1 Jan 2027** (S3, S4, S6, S26) **[sourced; verify who supervises DOS after 2027]**. **New and relevant:** EN 301 549 **v4.1.1** was published on 2 Sept 2026 and moves the web baseline from WCAG 2.1 to **WCAG 2.2 AA**; it binds only once cited in the Official Journal (expected 16 Dec 2026, one source) (S23) **[sourced; single source]**. Our docs (`docs/compliance.md`, last verified 2026-09-30) still say "next revision expected": update them, then this is our strongest timely hook: "we already target 2.2". Tailwind: the Commission's open-source strategy (3 June 2026) and the digital-sovereignty debate favour open, EU-controlled components (S24, S25) **[sourced]**; a no-telemetry, no-third-party-call library fits that story **[assumption that it persuades]**.

## 5. Developer praise: what earns it, what loses it

**Earns it** (observed in write-ups; most are practitioner blogs, not HN threads, so strength is moderate):

- Strict, correct behaviour with a design they can still bend; but verbosity and strictness are the complaints about the most thorough library (S36).
- Honest guidance on what the library cannot do: page-level navigation, wrapper handlers that break keyboard support, tooltip labelling (S38, S42).
- Publishing per-component status, including "not tested" (S34).
- Types by inference, tiny footprint, native HTML first, escape hatches (hooks, `as`, copy the theme).
- Copy-in (shadcn style) is praised for ownership and criticised because fixes do not arrive through `npm update` (S37); an installable package with hooks gives both.
- **Loses it:** absolute claims ("fully accessible"), testimonials standing in for evidence, a licence that surprises them, a stack that seems abandoned (S37), marketing before docs. The AGPL itself is read as a "non-starter" in many companies, and some policies forbid it outright (S27): the licence line must be honest, short and early. **[sourced]**

**First 10 seconds, in order** (the hero must show these without scrolling):

| ------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |

## 7. Licensing communication

**Say it in three plain lines, and link LICENSING.md.** (Draft wording; the lawyer approves before publishing.)

| You are                                                                                                                 | What applies (from LICENSING.md)                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| An individual, for yourself: hobby, learning, personal project, unpaid and not for any organisation                     | Free. You need not publish your own work under the AGPL                                       |
| Working for or in an organisation (company, non-profit, public body, school), for payment, or as a freelancer or agency | You need a commercial licence. It also covers support terms                                   |
| Anyone who modifies KvirnUI itself                                                                                      | The AGPL applies to KvirnUI and your changes: keep notices, offer the source of those changes |

**Attractive and honest framing.** (1) Open: the source is public (AGPL), readable by any auditor, and stays available; this is the continuity answer to "what if the maintainer stops?" **[assumption that it reassures; the AGPL side stays whatever happens]**. (2) Sustainable: a paid licence is what pays for the accessibility work and the testing; it is how a small team can promise a support period later. (3) Simple: no per-seat audit language until the owner chooses it. Never write "free for the public sector"; never imply the AGPL alone suffices for an organisation (LICENSING.md says otherwise). Do not publish prices or terms until decided; "Contact us" with `mailto:magnus@vike.se` is acceptable and has no third-party request. Dual-licensing practice: sell what the buyer cares about (no obligations, support, early access, a warranty), and own the copyright to be able to license at all (S28); our contribution clause already gives the maintainer a relicensing right **[sourced]**.

**Questions for the owner (licence).**

1. Has a lawyer reviewed LICENSING.md? It says "Draft: not yet reviewed". Does "any other use needs a commercial licence" fit the AGPL, which lets any user run the code under its own terms? The AGPL's section 7 additional permission is stated; the broader restriction for organisations needs a lawyer to reconcile it (this is a legal question, we only flag it).
2. What do you want an organisation to be able to do _without_ paying: evaluate, pilot, test? For how long, and in what words?
3. Price basis: per developer (as some commercial libraries publish, S29), per organisation, per project/client, or a flat annual fee that a public budget can plan? Do you publish numbers?
4. Support: response times, scope, how long a release line is supported (the vision's LTS), and what "support terms" means in the licence.
5. For agencies: one licence for all client projects, or per client? May the client use the delivered build without its own licence?
6. Can a public body buy by direct award, under a framework, or only through a tender? Which entity (company, tax number, VAT, invoicing) sells?
7. Do you offer a pilot or a design-partner licence (free or discounted, in exchange for a reference and feedback)?
8. Continuity: are you willing to publish a plain continuity statement (what happens to licensees if the project stops)?
9. Is a Swedish-law and Swedish/Finnish/Norwegian-language licence text needed for public buyers?
10. Will you accept contributions from licensees, and is the contribution clause (relicensing right) clear to them?
